::: key
**The project in one line.** A studio has to decide *before filming* how much to spend, on what kind of film, with whom, and when to release it. We use about 3,600 films to show which of those choices raise expected box office, which raise or lower the risk of a flop, and what slate of films a studio should build given how much risk it can carry.

**A first look at the data supports this** (The Numbers, 4,952 films 1980-2018): films over \$120M lose money less often than films under \$20M (40% vs 62% below break-even), and horror, not romance, is the cheap high-upside genre (median 2.75x budget, top 10% above 16x).
:::

# 1. The problem

How should a film studio choose budget, genre, source material, talent and release date so that it gets the best expected return for the risk it can take?

Most student movie projects predict IMDb score or gross from everything in the file. We only use what the studio controls or knows *before the film is made*. Ratings, Facebook likes, opening weekend and theatre counts all happen after release, so they are out of the model (they can still appear in descriptive charts, labelled as such).

We measure two outcomes, because a studio cares about the average return and about how often it loses money:

| Outcome | Definition | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Gross multiple | worldwide gross / production budget | Return per dollar spent. Comparable across budget sizes |
| Flop | gross multiple < 2.5 | Downside risk. 2.5x is the industry rule of thumb for break-even: the studio keeps about half the box office, and marketing costs roughly as much as half the production budget again |

The 2.5x line is an assumption we state and justify in the report (the brief rewards this). We can show the main charts also hold at 1x and 3x.

Hypotheses to test, ranked by how good a chart they make:

1. Bigger budgets *lower* risk, not just raise revenue (early data says yes).
2. Romance is "free money" (early data says no: rom-com median 2.28x, 54% flop; horror beats it).
3. Releasing into a crowded month (many big films within 30 days) cuts the multiple.
4. A director or lead with a strong track record lowers the chance of a flop more than it raises the upside.
5. Big studios and small studios should rationally pick different slates, because they can carry different amounts of risk.

## Problems in the base data

1. **`gross` is US domestic only**, not worldwide. Worldwide comes from The Numbers or TMDB.
2. **Non-US budgets are in local currency.** *The Host* shows a budget of 12,215 (Korean won, millions). Use The Numbers' USD budget, or keep USA/UK films only.
3. **Facebook likes were collected around 2016**, years after most films came out. They measure fame earned partly *from* the film, so they leak the outcome. Out of the model.
4. 42 duplicated titles, 132 budgets under \$1M, broken characters in names (`Zoí‚ Kravitz`). Names need fixing before matching people across films.
5. The Numbers scrape has shifted columns for some films (text like "All Time Domestic Box Office for..." sits in the genre field). Fix by taking genre from the base table and TMDB.

## The joined table (one row per film)

| Group | Columns | Studio controls it? |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | title, release_date, year | |
| **Money** | budget_real (2016 \$), log_budget, budget_tier (<20M, 20-60M, 60-120M, 120M+) | Yes |
| **Content** | main_genre + genre dummies, story_source (original, book, comic, sequel, real events), production_method (live action, animation), content_rating, runtime | Yes |
| **Talent** | director and lead actor track record: number of prior films, median prior multiple, prior flop rate | Yes (who to hire) |
| **Studio** | production company / distributor, its prior film count, median multiple and flop rate | Partly |
| **Timing** | release month, holiday window, `competitors_30d`, `big_competitors_30d` (budget at least as large), `same_genre_30d`, `competitor_budget_sum_30d` | Yes |

A person's or studio's track record uses only films released *before* the target film. Otherwise the model knows the future.

Competition counts use release dates from TMDB and The Numbers together (about 7,000 films). That list undercounts small releases, which we state as a limitation.

# 5. Models

| Stage | Model | Target | Tool | Why this one |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diagnostic | Linear regression (OLS) | log gross multiple | statsmodels | Coefficients read as "% change in return", which is what the coefficient plot shows |
| Diagnostic | Logistic regression | flop (yes/no) | sklearn `LogisticRegression` | Same choices, but on risk instead of average return |
| Predictive | Gradient boosting with quantile loss | 10th, 50th, 90th percentile of log multiple | sklearn `GradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile")` | Gives a range per film, not one number. Risk is the width of that range |
| Predictive baseline | Median multiple of the film's genre x budget tier cell | same | Polars | The model has to beat the simple rule a studio already uses |
| Prescriptive | Monte Carlo slate simulation | total slate profit | numpy | Turns predicted ranges into a decision. Brief's own example 4 uses Monte Carlo |

Validation: train on films released up to 2011, test once on 2012-2016 (2019 where The Numbers covers it). A time split, because the studio always predicts the future from the past. Tuning inside the training set with 5-fold cross-validation and `GridSearchCV`, as in Lec9.

The prescriptive step: a studio has, say, \$300M to spend in a year. It can fund one \$150M tentpole plus three \$50M films, or ten \$30M horror and comedy films, and so on. For each candidate slate we draw 10,000 outcomes from the predicted ranges, convert gross to studio profit with the 2.5x rule, and record the expected profit and the chance the whole slate loses money. Plotting every slate gives a risk-return frontier, like a stock portfolio. A big studio that can absorb a bad year picks a different point on it than a small one that cannot.

# 6. Limitations to state up front

1. Box office is not profit. Streaming, TV and home video revenue are missing, and so are real marketing budgets. The 2.5x rule stands in for both.
2. Actor and director pay is not public. Track record is a proxy for star power, not a salary.
3. Genre is a rough proxy for effects spending, not a measure of it.
4. Survivorship: the datasets favour films big enough to be tracked. Tiny films that vanished are underrepresented, which makes the low-budget tier look better than it is.
5. Associations, not causes. A big budget may lower flop risk because studios only give big budgets to projects they already trust.
